Podcast / Origin Story

The first episode: three petrolheads, one table

Andre Roux on hitting 138 km/h on a downhill skateboard, Leon Bosman's road from toy cars to national television, and the eight-year build behind Peter's own Porsche Carrera RSR Turbo tribute.

Peter van der Spuy Adapted from the podcast's pilot episode, with Leon Bosman and Andre Roux

This was the pilot, the first time Peter van der Spuy, Leon Bosman and Andre Roux sat down at the same table to record. Introducing himself, Peter told the story of standing up at a friend's 600-guest wedding and opening with "for those who don't know who I am, my name is Peter van der Spuy," a line that stuck so well the local Porsche club later had a badge made for him with the joke printed on it.

138 km/h on a skateboard

Andre Roux got into filming by shooting a friend skateboarding downhill, and ended up strapping on a full leather race suit and full-face helmet himself, reaching speeds around 100 km/h and finishing sixth overall in a South African downhill series. He estimates he has broken nearly every bone on the left side of his body, the side that takes the impact going downhill. The rider he filmed most, Dino Lorenzo, won the national championship every year he competed and climbed to eighth in the world before funding ran out, and Roux watched him hit 138 km/h on a public road at Rist Canyon in Colorado. Downhill boards are built nothing like a street deck, with the front trucks angled 40 to 45 degrees and the rear as shallow as 15 degrees purely for stability at speed.

"There's no room for error. None whatsoever."

From toy cars at 4am to national television

Leon Bosman traces his own obsession to being nine or ten years old, riding in his cousin's four-door Corolla SR5 fitted with dual 40mm side-draft Weber carburetors, and the moment his cousin floored it onto the highway. He later ran a tuning workshop, picked up a camera almost as an afterthought, and was spotted by a DStv producer at a car show after mentioning a pilot episode he'd shot with a single friend. Encouraged by that same cousin, who died shortly afterward, he entered and won a presenter search, then fronted around fifteen to twenty episodes of motoring television, a stint as a Harley-Davidson brand ambassador, and a year and a half co-hosting a local radio show. The work put him in the same circles as figures like New Zealand drift driver Mad Mike Whiddett and actor Adam Ferrara, a former host of Top Gear USA, connections he traces directly back to that first pilot episode shot with a single friend and a camera.

A squash court in Moscow

Peter traces his own instinct for car control directly back to the cockpit rather than to any racing pedigree. Close to thirty years of flying Gulfstreams has meant roughly fifty simulator sessions across his career, two a year at flight-safety training centres in Atlanta, where the aircraft are built, and elsewhere around the world, drilling the same fine-margin precision, centreline discipline and speed control that a racing line also demands; a Gulfstream's takeoff speed, he points out, lands close to what a car is doing down Kyalami's back straight, around 250 km/h. He came to competitive racing with zero background in it. Fellow Porsche owner Andre Bedenot spotted him comfortably beating GT3 drivers at a Porsche Club cone-and-timing event in an old 996 Turbo, offered to train him and lend him a race car, and entered him, reluctantly, into his first real circuit race around 2012 or 2013. Peter won his class on debut, a result Bedenot, with 25 years of racing behind him, said he'd never seen a first-timer manage.

The technique that made it possible has an unlikely origin. Peter left-foot brakes, a habit that shaves a second or two off most laps, learned not on a track but after snapping his Achilles tendon on a squash court in Moscow, treated on the spot by court-side medics he only half-jokingly compares to rusty needles and staple guns. A doctor told him he'd be off driving entirely for four months; he was bedridden in pain for the first week alone. Left-foot braking was the workaround that let him keep racing with one foot effectively out of action, and the habit simply stuck once the injury healed.

An eight-year tribute build

Peter's own contribution to the table was his tribute to the 1974 Porsche Carrera RSR Turbo, the first turbocharged car the factory built, raced at Le Mans that year before an electrical fault in the pits cost it the overall lead. Only four originals exist, now trading for up to $8 million each. Peter's version took eight years, and the engine alone went through South African builder after South African builder before it would even survive a dyno run, breaking a rocker on one attempt and blowing its rings on another, engines that never made it anywhere near the track. The one engine that did make it out still couldn't hold its cooling under load until the team reverted to the same style of cooling arrangement Porsche used on the period-correct 935, paired with a freshly rebuilt engine from the United States: Chris Carroll's Turbocraft, who built him the 31st engine to that exact specification, a 996 Turbo 3.6-litre block reworked with racing components. Road tune runs about 500 to 550 horsepower at 0.5 bar boost, with headroom to around 800 horsepower at 1.1 to 1.2 bar and an overtake-style boost button on top of that. The car itself weighs roughly 1,200 kilograms against the original's 900, so Peter tunes toward the upper end of that range specifically to keep the power-to-weight feel close to the genuine article, running unusually wide period tyres and a suspension setup that only came together once he switched to components from the US air-cooled specialist Elephant Racing. He has since raced it at Kyalami in the Sports and GT Racing series, beating the current cup cars outright.

The skateboard clip that went round the world

Andre's first real brush with the film industry came from a single clip, not a plan. Riding in a car alongside Dino Lorenzo at close range for a wide, immersive angle, he filmed a downhill run that went properly viral, picked up by CNN, the BBC, Sky News and outlets well beyond motoring or extreme-sports media. The clip was later hit with a copyright claim on YouTube from Iran's state broadcasting authority attempting to collect its ad revenue, genuine enough that Andre pulled the video down himself rather than let the claim stand. Skateboarding in South Africa has faded since, and Andre stepped back from it himself after a serious crash a few years ago that fully separated his elbow, an injury that left him with permanent plates and screws and a sport he says he still misses.
