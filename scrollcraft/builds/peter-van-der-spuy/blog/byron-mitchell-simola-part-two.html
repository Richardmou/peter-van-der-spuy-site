King of the Hill at Simola, part two

Fresh off a Simola Hillclimb win, Peter and Byron Mitchell revisit the 2023 endurance campaign they ran together: a broken suspension bracket with 45 minutes left, a burnt arm, and a podium nobody expected.

Peter van der Spuy Adapted from the podcast episode, with Byron Mitchell

Byron Mitchell returns fresh off winning King of the Hill at the Simola Hillclimb, but most of this conversation goes backward, first to the hillclimb campaign that built to that win, then to the endurance season the two of them ran together the year before. Peter has told the stats-forward version of the endurance story elsewhere on this site: third in Class A across the 2023 Southern African Endurance Series, in a privateer Juno SS3 run by Dolphin Engineering. This is the version with the actual damage report.

Half a tenth

Simola has grown into one of South African motorsport's biggest single-day draws, pulling an estimated 35,000 spectators to the mountain in a recent year alone, with individual event videos racking up views in the tens of thousands online. Byron first entered Simola in 2021 and won his class outright. He won his class again in 2022 and finished fourth outright, a result he calls the most fun his team has ever had at the event: a duel with a competitor driving an identical car, same engine, same everything, down to which of them had a straight-pipe exhaust for a louder note. That rival now races internationally in Porsche Cup competition, which gives some sense of the level Byron was matching lap for lap that weekend. Into the class final, Byron's personal best stood at 39.90 seconds. He ran a 39.84. His rival ran a 39.80, four hundredths quicker, but clipped a cone on the way down and had the time deleted under the rules. The class was Byron's.

That set up the King of the Hill shootout, the outright final against every class winner on the mountain. On the start line, his father, in Byron's headset as chief engineer, told him plainly he didn't care if the car came back in pieces: he needed one more tenth, whatever it cost. Byron gave him a tenth and a half, a 39.70. His rival ran 39.75 immediately after. Half a tenth separated the two of them for the class podium that day, decided in front of a crowd the organisers estimated at 35,000 people. The following year, 2023, delivered the opposite lesson. On the line for the class final, with the same rivalry building to the same pitch, rain arrived and a camshaft position sensor failed, dropping the car into limp mode before it could run. Byron didn't make either final that year. Peter wasn't at the track for it and got the news by phone. "Motorsport is brutal," is how Byron sums it up. "You win some, you lose some." The 2025 result that opens this conversation, a King of the Hill win, is the payoff for staying with it through that stretch.

A budget a twentieth the size

Byron is blunt about the gap in resources. Competing against factory-backed budgets north of 20 million rand for the season, the team Byron's father ran spent under a million rand across the whole year, finishing fifth overall in the Nine Hours, the season's last and double-points round, and still beat cars driven by the likes of Stuart White and Nick Adcock over the full championship, in part because both drivers missed races: White failed to make pit exit within five minutes of the Nine Hours finish, and Adcock skipped rounds for international commitments elsewhere. "You've got to be in it to win it," Byron says. The team's own strategy shifted over the season from chasing outright pace to a simpler goal: finish every round, and the points would take care of themselves.

Forty-five minutes, one broken bracket

The Nine Hours of Kyalami very nearly ended in retirement. A Cobra spun into contact with Byron's car, breaking the rear-left lower suspension bracket clean, and left the suspension hanging loose against two exhaust pipes too hot to touch by hand. The crew judged the fix would take 30 minutes they didn't have; with 45 minutes left on the clock and the car running third, Peter carried the transponder up to race control to formally withdraw.

"All you need to do is finish this race, and you'll get a third place."

Race control's math was simple: enough of the field had its own problems that the team, having already covered the required percentage of race distance, only needed to cross the finish line to keep its position. Peter went back to the pits with the news, and the crew dove into the repair anyway, heat and all. Byron and a crewmate, Jonty, stripped the broken bracket off while another team member ran an airline and a spray bottle over the exhaust trying to bring the temperature down just enough to work near it. The final nut wouldn't thread by hand; Jonty's fingers were already burning against the pipe when Byron took over, held his bare hand against the hot metal, and forced it on. He still carries the mark on his arm. Suit and helmet went back on within seconds of the nut being tightened, and the car rolled out of the pits with only a few seconds to spare before the cutoff, wheel alignment knocked out and a control arm still bent, running roughly a minute a lap off its normal pace for what remained of the race. The car finished. The podium held. It was the team's first trophy of the season; a win at Red Star followed not long after.

Byron is pointed about what that year taught him, and about what he sees as a gap between himself and a lot of drivers coming up through the sport now: many, in his telling, get in a car a team has prepared for them, drive, and go home without knowing what's actually underneath them. He can name every bolt on his own Juno, because he and his father's team build and maintain the car themselves. That's the throughline between both halves of this conversation, a hillclimb run decided in tenths of a second and a nine-hour race saved with a bare hand on a hot exhaust pipe: neither result happens without someone who actually understands what's underneath the bodywork when it goes wrong, a lesson that started on a Friday evening at a go-kart track and never really stopped applying.
