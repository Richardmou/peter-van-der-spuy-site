Born to race: Byron Mitchell's journey, part one

Quad-bike racing from age four, a grandfather who raced the Isle of Man, and the day Byron Mitchell's one-footed engineer father beat both his sons at go-karts.

Peter van der Spuy Adapted from the podcast episode, with Byron Mitchell

Byron Mitchell's racing career exists because of a loophole. His grandfather raced motorbikes, competing at the Isle of Man, and after his share of bad crashes there, Byron's grandmother banned anything on two wheels from the family for good. Byron's father found the workaround: quad bikes. At three years old, Byron and his brother, four years his senior, were each given identical 50cc quads.

Racing before school

The rules required racers to be at least four, so Byron waited a year while his brother started without him. He won his first off-road championship at six, two years after he began, in a class where every rider ran the same lightly regulated 50cc machines, tracks kept deliberately small with parents jogging alongside the bikes in case a child came off. From there the progression was steady: 50cc to 100cc, then 250cc, then the 450s, regarded in quad racing as the top spec, effectively the class's Formula 1 equivalent, off limits until age sixteen. At ten, his brother had a serious fall at a track in Philadelphia, outside Cape Town, riding flat out into a sandstorm so thick that visibility dropped to about 20 meters. He went straight over an obstacle he never saw, was thrown over the handlebars with his wrists caught in the clutch and brake levers, and broke both of them clean. Byron's father, who raced quads himself at the time, called it quits for the whole family on the spot. Quad racing stopped for a year or two, and Byron's brother never got back on a bike.

The year of the Roof of Africa

Byron pushed to return. A friend named Tyron, racing in the same age group, had just moved up to a 250cc machine, and watching him go was enough to pull Byron back in. His father called a family vote: his brother was firmly out, his mother wasn't keen, his father himself was undecided, and Byron was, in his own words, all for it. His father bought him a full race-spec Yamaha YZ250, suspension and cams included, and they were back racing within the year.

The season that followed is the one Byron calls the highlight of his entire off-road career. The family travelled the country for it, including a race outside Kimberley run in what he describes as hurricane-force sandstorm conditions, one of the most dangerous events he's ridden. The same year, at twelve, he rode the Junior Roof of Africa, the youth edition of South Africa's best-known off-road motorcycle enduro, held annually in the mountains of Lesotho. The format ran on a two-hour clock, riders released roughly twenty seconds apart onto a lap circuit, the winner decided first by whoever completed the most laps inside the time limit, and then, among riders tied on laps, by whoever posted the faster cumulative time. There was no navigator and no GPS: the route was marked with orange stickers nailed to trees, always kept on the rider's left to confirm the correct direction, three in a row warning of a left turn ahead and two marking the turn itself, plus pink spray-paint arrows on open ground done a week before the event, which livestock and weather had usually half-erased by race day. Ten minutes into the first lap, the entire field, more than 200 riders, arrived at an open field and collectively lost the trail at the same moment. Byron spotted the next marker first and tried to bluff the riders around him by riding toward it slowly, planning to accelerate once he was close enough that no one would catch him; several others had already clocked the same marker and simply overtook him anyway. The course crossed four rivers, one deep enough that his quad floated and had to be steered by thrust alone, drifting downstream before finding the far bank. He finished sixth overall out of more than 200 entrants, his friend third. It was, by his account, one of the toughest single races he's ever done, and afterward he told his father he felt saturated, that he'd achieved what he'd set out to prove and had no interest in chasing it further. That result closed out his off-road career before the pivot to circuit racing that followed.

The day his father beat him

Byron's father is an engineer, and not by coincidence. In his twenties, on a road bike, he lost his footing on wet tar, hit a concrete parking bollard, and nearly lost his foot, left hanging on by a tendon in the crash. He kept riding and racing quads afterward regardless, on a custom lengthened boot he engineered himself in his own shop to work around the injury, since a standard boot wouldn't flex the way the joint now needed.

The pivot into circuit racing came at 13, almost 14, on an ordinary Friday evening at the go-kart track at Canal Walk in Cape Town. Byron and his brother, confident they'd beat their father easily, needled him into a race. He beat them both.

"He gave us a hiding. He's a man with a mission."

Byron credits his father as the reason he kept progressing at all: total support for whatever sport either son wanted to try, tennis and golf and rugby included, with time and money scaling up only once a son showed he was serious about it. They were never the wealthiest family in the karting paddock, up against competitors who could replace tyres and chassis every weekend, but his father found what he could when it mattered. Years later, watching a rival's Formula Vee at the track, Byron told his father he wished he could drive one himself; it was well outside what the family could afford at the time. Three or four years after that conversation, his father bought him one, a used car in no real racing condition, and the two of them stripped it down to the bare chassis and rebuilt it from scratch together in the same shop. That car is still the one Byron races today, and his father, still doing the engineering himself, is the person Byron now calls his chief engineer on every endurance weekend. Canal Walk was the night circuit racing became that sport. Part two picks up from there, through to King of the Hill at Simola.
