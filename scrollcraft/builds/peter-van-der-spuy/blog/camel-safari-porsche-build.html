Porsche / Safari Projek

The Camel Safari Porsche Projek

Africa's wildest Porsche 911 build: how a converted 996 Turbo became, as far as anyone in the group can tell, the first Porsche product ever driven down the Swartberg Pass's Hell, and back out again.

Peter van der Spuy Adapted from the podcast episode

In 2017, Peter van der Spuy flew to Savannah, Georgia for a Gulfstream recurrent training course. He came home with an idea for a car.

A friend in Savannah had a classic Porsche rally car in Martini livery, converted for dirt himself, and took Peter out on the back roads around the city. Peter, a lifelong Porsche owner who has never stuck with one car for long, had never driven one off-road before. "I was hooked immediately," he says. He thought: we have to build this back home.

Why a 996, and why Camel

Back in South Africa, Peter approached Johan de Bruyn, a friend he had raced alongside for years, about converting a Porsche for the dirt. The idea stalled on one problem: the obvious donor cars, the air-cooled classics, were already too expensive to risk on gravel. A clean classic was running 2 to 3 million rand before a single modification, against roughly $200,000 for an equivalent build in the United States.

The 996, Porsche's first water-cooled 911, built from the late 1990s to around 2004, solved it. It never had the classic's reputation, and it was still picking up 911s and 911 Turbos for a fraction of the price, roughly 500,000 rand to a million rand at the time. Mechanically it was proven and bulletproof. As a donor car for something that was going to get dented, scratched and buried in sand, it made far more sense than a car worth ten times as much.

The Camel Trophy livery came for two reasons. The base color, a sandy Sahara sand, was chosen because the car was going to be permanently coated in dust and needed a paint job that would hide it rather than show every grain. And the Camel Trophy itself, the old Land Rover and Range Rover expeditions that ran the length of Africa through the 1980s and 90s, was the reference point Peter grew up watching. Putting that livery on a Porsche, a marque with its own deep rally history, Safari rallies and Dakar included, was, in his words, "a bit bold," but it made the car instantly recognizable.

"This is the best off-road car he's ever built."

Building it

The company that grew out of this, Safari Projek, builds its cars from 996 and 997-generation Carrera and Turbo donors. The single biggest piece of engineering is the suspension: race engineer Philip Fisser spent roughly two and a half years developing a setup that pushes the track five centimeters wider than stock and delivers around 200 millimeters of travel, using three-way adjustable shocks from Reiger, a name well known in rally-raid circles, built to the exact weight and travel spec of each car. The control arms are machined from 7075 aircraft aluminum. According to the team, no Safari Projek 911 has ever had a suspension failure across everything the fleet has covered.

Peter's own car, a 2002 996 Turbo that had previously been a concours-condition car in his garage, became the company's first turbocharged build. It carried serial number 25, meaning 24 cars had already gone through the workshop before his. Turning a turbo car into an off-roader created its own problems: the full-length underbody protection plate needed to route cooling air around the turbos properly, which meant redesigning the exhaust system from front to back (the standard exhaust on these cars weighs around 40 kilograms, and the redesign also freed up space and cut weight). The build took about eight months.

Every car that leaves the workshop also gets a full custom front and rear bumper with integrated underbody protection running the length of the car, carbon fiber trim, wheel arch extensions to square up the wider track front to rear, a Thule roof rack and spare wheel mount (redesigned after the original brackets weren't strong enough), and a limited-slip differential. Peter's car runs Recaro seats, a custom roll bar (more stiffening than rollover protection, since the cars sit low and the team has never had one flip), and Falken tires, the same tire the Safari Projek team has raced on in the South African Rally Raid Championship. A basic build, suspension, bumpers, underbody protection and exhaust, is the non-negotiable floor; everything from there, wheels, interior, wrap color, is built to the owner's spec. At the time of the episode, a build on a 997.2 GTS donor was quoted at around 2.6 million rand, separate from the donor car itself, against roughly 6 to 7 million rand for Porsche's own factory 911 Dakar.

The Hell, and back

After Peter's car was finished, the team put it through a 300-kilometer test drive before handing it over. Once he had it, Peter organized a group rally: 17 to 18 of the roughly 25 Safari Projek cars built at that stage ran a six to seven day route through the back roads of the Western Cape.

That was the shakedown. The real test came afterward, when Peter set out alone down a track in the Swartberg Pass, near Prince Albert, known locally as the Hell: roughly 48 to 50 kilometers one-way into a remote valley, regarded as one of the most rugged 4x4 passes in the region, normally a three to four hour drive each way. Word among the Safari Projek owners was that no Porsche product had ever been down it. That was enough to put it on Peter's list.

He went in solo, with one non-driving passenger along for company and a camera to record it, no backup vehicle. There was rock crawling in first gear for long stretches, sections fast enough to test the cooling properly, and, by his account, zero overheating problems the whole way down. Two other vehicles attempting the same route that day, a Range Rover Sport and a stock Land Cruiser, both got stuck and had to be towed out.

"A Porsche 911 is appearing."

At the bottom, a group with lifted, off-road-shod Land Cruisers watched the Camel-liveried 911 pull in and, in Peter's telling, could not quite believe it. The woman who runs the small accommodation at the bottom of the pass, a fixture there for years, told him she had never seen a Porsche come down before. Peter turned around and drove back out the same day, arriving with scrapes underneath but nothing broken.

Since then, the car has been to Botswana, Eswatini and Lesotho. Safari Projek has gone on to build more than 45 cars, taken a group of 19 of them on a trip covering just over 2,500 kilometers without a single problem on the 911s, and started converting Porsche Cayennes, including V6 and V8 diesel models with a 2-inch lift and 2 inches of extra suspension travel, as a more practical, higher-capacity companion to the 911 build. Peter still wants to see one more thing happen: a Porsche factory Dakar, taken down the Hell and back. So far, nobody has taken him up on it.
