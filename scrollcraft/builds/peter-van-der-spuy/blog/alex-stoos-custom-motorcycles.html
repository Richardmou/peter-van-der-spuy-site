Builders / Motorcycles

From jet skis to world-class custom motorcycles

Stoos Customs founder Alex Stoos on the craft behind a custom build, a farm childhood of things with motors, and the day he mistook a Vikings star for a stranger in his own workshop.

Peter van der Spuy Adapted from the podcast episode, with Alex Stoos

Alex Stoos, founder of Stoos Customs, is particular about the word used to describe what he does. "Not a builder," his friend and co-host Leon Bosman insists, "a craftsman." Stoos agrees: "I don't just assemble. I design, manufacture, bleed, sweat." He'd cut his hand on a machine earlier that same afternoon, which he treats as simply part of the job.

Anything with a motor

Stoos grew up on a farm and got his first bike, a Honda 70 three-wheeler he calls "an absolute death trap," at four years old. A two-wheeled KX65 dirt bike followed, then a period racing boats that developed into freestyle jet skiing, competitive enough that he credits it with teaching him the nerve to flip a jet ski upside down on purpose. What actually pointed him at motorcycles specifically was Discovery Channel's Biker Build-Off, watched as a teenager who'd always tested better at the creative side of school than anything academic: seeing builders like Indian Larry and Russell Mitchell fabricate a bike entirely by hand, rather than assemble factory parts, was the moment he realised it was a real trade, not just a hobby of fixing other people's discarded machinery. Up to that point he hadn't considered being a technician a genuine career option at all; what drew him in wasn't prestige but the specific satisfaction of taking something broken, someone else's trash, and bringing it back to life. A friend from the jet-ski circuit told him about MMI, a motorcycle trade school in the United States, and he emigrated to train there for Honda and Harley-Davidson.

That same friend's connection to Russell Mitchell, owner of the well-known Los Angeles custom shop Exile Cycles, got Stoos an apprenticeship there while he lived in North Hollywood, which is where his own fabrication style actually took shape. It ended the way a lot of American visas end: his ran out, immigration told him to leave, and he fought to stay before accepting it as, in his own words, a blessing. He came home and started Stoos Customs from scratch thirteen years ago.

World class, but better known overseas

Momentum built slowly at first: a beach photoshoot of a BMW R18 build that Leon shot and that later ran in the custom-motorcycle site Pipe Burn, then, roughly three years before this conversation, a second invitation to Austin's Handbuilt Show after Alan from Revival Cycles saw one of his builds and asked him to bring it over. Leon is blunt about the domestic reception by comparison: he considers Stoos's work good enough to examine with a magnifying glass and find nothing out of place, yet locally the recognition lags well behind what the same bikes get once they leave the country. Both men see the same pattern in other corners of South African build culture, Peter's own Camel Safari Porsche project among them: work that reads as a curiosity at home is judged world-class the moment it's compared against international peers. Stoos's own theory is less about talent than exposure. A self-taught builder working alone in a garage, with no YouTube tutorials or Instagram feeds to benchmark against, has nothing to tell him his best work isn't actually his best work; Stoos credits starting his own career inside a shop with a zero-tolerance standard for teaching him to be, in his own description, pedantic and often overcritical of his own builds.

Leon's read on the local scene is that there's no shortage of raw skill, just too few places where all of it ends up under one roof. "There's always a good guy in every workshop," he says, "but if they could only just get all those good guys under one roof, that's really what you need to build a really good product." He sees the same split he does in custom bikes playing out in South African motorsport builds generally: plenty of individually talented fabricators scattered across small operations, and comparatively few shops with the full team, and the full standard, to build something that reads as finished rather than merely functional the moment it leaves the country.

The homeless-looking guy in the workshop

Leon tells the story of photographing one of Stoos's builds when a stranger rode up on an old Harley and sat down about ten metres away, waiting for Stoos. "He looked homeless," Leon says, unassuming, road-worn, clearly a man who'd spent real hours behind a machine. They ended up sitting on the workshop floor together, mopping up oil, talking for two or three hours. It was only on the drive home that Leon placed the face: Travis Fimmel, star of Vikings, who had lived across the road from Stoos a few years earlier while filming Raised by Wolves. The two had originally met through a mutual friend at Exile Cycles in California, where Fimmel used to run in the same Hollywood circles before relocating next door to Stoos, unannounced and unbothered by who knew it.

"I didn't know who he was. We were just getting drunk and drinking beers."

A titanium exhaust and a blown budget

One recent build, entered into a themed build-off displayed at Simola Hillclimb, set its own rules: the base bike had to be a 1981 Harley Shovelhead, and it had to run carbon wheels. Everything past those two constraints was open, so Stoos went full specification, partnering with a machining company called Speed and Science to CNC the barrels and heads from aircraft-grade billet aluminium and speccing full suspension and carbon BST wheels. The exhaust was where the build nearly broke him: he wanted it in titanium, and no South African supplier could provide the specific grade in the length he needed. Importing it was the only option, and buying it by the length rather than the exact piece would have pushed the cost to nearly 50,000 rand on its own. He went over budget on the build regardless, on a project with no fixed ceiling to begin with, simply because getting the right material into the country was that difficult. The finished bike went on display at Simola Hillclimb, tucked under a tent that took Peter himself a genuine search to find, alongside a matching build from his friend and fellow fabricator Joe, run through the same Speed and Science partnership.
