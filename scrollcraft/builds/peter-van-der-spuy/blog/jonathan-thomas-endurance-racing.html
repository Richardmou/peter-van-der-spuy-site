Motorsport / Endurance

From go-karts to the pinnacle of endurance racing

Jonathan Thomas on the go-kart test that hooked him at sixteen, the father who chose to fund a racing career over a house, and what it costs to rent a seat in Europe's prototype series.

Peter van der Spuy Adapted from the podcast episode, with Jonathan Thomas

Jonathan Thomas came to racing with no family history in it and a late start by karting standards. There were dirt bikes from about six or seven years old, nothing more purposeful than that, until a school friend who karted competitively talked him into a test day at sixteen. One session in a Senior Max kart was enough. He went home and told his father they were selling all three of his dirt bikes to buy a go-kart and do it properly. Four months and one race later, he finished second in the Clubman series on his debut, then kept climbing through Western Province and national classes, racing DD2 gearbox karts by the time he reached the top of the national ladder.

A father who financed racing instead of a house

Thomas's father runs Partners Hair Design, a Cape Town chain that has grown to 22 stores and 220 staff, along with a training academy and online store. As the racing budgets grew year on year, Peter recalls a conversation with him about the choice to keep backing his son rather than putting the money elsewhere.

"I'd rather finance the house and spend my money on racing."

That backing carried Thomas to a karting world finals in Italy in 2019 and a DD2 lap record at Killarney that stood for years, then into proper circuit racing: a Backdraft Cobra kit car at the Kyalami 9 Hour in 2020 that broke before the finish, and an ex-Pilbeam prototype in 2022 that was beaten only on reliability, not pace, by Peter's own crew that same weekend. It was in that 2022 race that Thomas overtook a Ligier on track driven by Nick Adcock, a competitor with 25 years of racing behind him. Adcock tracked him down afterward to ask who he was, and not long after that, asked if he wanted to race in Europe.

Platinum, gold, silver, bronze

European endurance racing ranks every driver on a four-tier scale, from bronze up through silver and gold to platinum, meant to separate wealthy enthusiasts from professionals and balance the field accordingly. Anyone who has competed at a karting world finals is automatically bumped straight to silver, which is how Thomas entered the system already ranked as a professional rather than a gentleman driver. Age also weighs on the classification: a driver's own pace can outrun their official rank, producing what the paddock calls a shiny bronze, an older amateur-rated driver who is genuinely as quick as the pros around him. The ranking has real financial teeth. A team fielding two silver-rated drivers, rather than a silver paired with a bronze, has to serve a longer mandatory pit stop, close to a minute extra per stop, because the field is presumed faster without a true amateur aboard. That penalty is also why bronze drivers, not the silvers or golds who actually win races, tend to pay the most to be in the car: their entry fee effectively subsidises the pace their more experienced teammates bring.

A debut win at Paul Ricard

His first session in a real European prototype, an LMP3-class car built around a stock Ford Coyote V8 producing roughly two tons of downforce, at Circuit Paul Ricard for the French team CD Sport, nearly ended his international career before it began. He came in from his first laps feeling car sick, unable to process the corner speeds, and told his girlfriend Zilka he couldn't do it. She talked him back into the car. By race day he'd found his footing enough to win on debut.

Zilka has since become central to how the team operates on a race weekend: she runs the travel and admin, and on race day sits trackside with a headset, tracking rival drivers' sector times to feed Thomas real-time comparisons the broadcast never shows. A single European race weekend has cost Thomas around 380,000 rand as a silver-rated driver, roughly 2 million rand across the six rounds of a full season.

A season of near misses

The following season was defined by how often the result nearly went Thomas's way rather than by how often it did. Paired with a 24-year-old French silver-rated teammate, the car led the first four hours of a six-hour race at Portimao before a power steering failure ended the run. At Paul Ricard, running second, the car's front nose clip failed and detached, a manufacturing fault that hit several cars that weekend, not just his. At Magny-Cours, leading in a storming rain so heavy the low-slung prototypes were aquaplaning at 80 km/h, the team also ran into a controversial mid-race rule change: with conditions bad enough to red-flag the session, officials suspended the usual rule requiring silver-rated drivers to hand over more of the driving time to their bronze teammates, a change that favoured teams without a silver-silver pairing like his, over a call Thomas still considers politically motivated. Only Hockenheim actually converted into a win. Through it all, Thomas says the team kept finishing on pace without the results to show it, a season of what-ifs rather than a bad one.

He is now in talks with CD Sport about a step up to the Michelin Le Mans Cup for the following year, weighing it against staying in the Ultimate Cup alongside Nick Adcock as the only two drivers in the car, a pairing that would avoid the silver-silver pit penalty altogether. Working the paddock, as Thomas describes it, has less to do with outright speed than most people assume: plenty of drivers are fast, but a bronze-rated teammate is choosing who to pay to sit beside as much as who to pay to win with, which makes coaching ability, clear feedback and simple personality fit as valuable to a silver-rated professional's career as lap time. "You're only as quick as your weakest link," is how he puts it, and getting a bronze teammate closer to his own pace matters more to the team's results than protecting his own numbers ever would. It's a lesson that runs the opposite direction from where his own career started, learning to read feedback from a mechanic or his father rather than give it, and one he says he wouldn't have understood properly at sixteen, back when the only feedback that mattered was his own lap time.
