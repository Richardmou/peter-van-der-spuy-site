Conservation / Wildlife

The 900-hectare sanctuary built for one blind rhino

Simon Borchett, co-founder of the Shannon Elizabeth Foundation, on Munu, a blind black rhino bull rescued from Addo, and the fight to save a subspecies down to about a thousand animals.

Peter van der Spuy Adapted from the podcast episode, with Simon Borchett

Simon Borchett grew up inside conservation rather than discovering it later. His father published and edited some of the continent's most notable wildlife books and magazines, including a stint as chief editor of Africa Geographic, and by retirement had over 500 titles to his name. As a kid, while friends went to beach houses for the school holidays, Borchett was dragged along to elephant orphanages and three-day stints in bird hides with wildlife photographers. He didn't appreciate it at the time. Borchett detoured into advertising and illustration, working as a creative director for years in what he now calls "a highly manipulative business," before wildlife photography pulled him back. It was through that work that he met Shannon Elizabeth, the American actress who had left Hollywood to build a life around African conservation. Together they founded the Shannon Elizabeth Foundation, and pulled Borchett's own father out of retirement to chair it.

A camera built to tell a story

Borchett is specific about what separates a wildlife photograph worth looking at from one that isn't. Anyone can point a decent camera at a lion or a leopard and get lucky with a portrait, he says; the difference is understanding the animal well enough to photograph what it's doing and why, the character behind the species rather than the species itself. He compares it to a striking car photograph, or a song that can stop time and transport you somewhere else the moment you hear it: without a story behind the image, it's just a picture of an animal.

His clearest example is an afternoon at Addo Elephant National Park, watching a mother reunite with her herd after giving birth, introducing the newborn calf. He stayed with that herd for around five hours. The young males, the "askaris," formed a loose protective line around the group, facing outward toward the parked vehicles. One by one, the other cars left. Borchett stayed, and watched the mother and calf walk ahead of the herd while the rest fanned out to match the baby's pace, the whole group pausing whenever it stopped to rest. When the herd finally moved off, he had to guess which of two exit tracks they'd take; he chose one, parked, and waited. The mother walked past him close enough to touch, glanced over, and carried on. Later the bull came alongside the vehicle and put his trunk through the window, looked around the cabin, and moved on. Borchett describes it as a conscious decision on the animal's part about whether you're a threat, not luck: a kind of mutual respect.

Getting that kind of shot takes patience most people underestimate. On one trip through the Okavango with safari guide Rob Barber, the two of them spent six and a half hours easing a vehicle roughly 250 metres toward a skittish young leopard with her first litter, tacking sideways in short five-metre bursts rather than approaching her directly, reading the terrain around the campfire the night before to plan the route. That is what a single frame can cost: hours of travel, the cost of the equipment, and no guarantee the shot exists until you're back looking at the files.

A blind bull named Munu

The foundation's clearest project began with a rhino found walking in circles in Addo Elephant National Park, malnourished and marked by hyenas. Rangers darted him and discovered he was completely blind, the result of an old territorial fight. Munu is a black rhino bull of the southwestern desert subspecies, historically ranging across Angola, Botswana, Namibia and the western half of South Africa. That subspecies is now extinct in Angola and Botswana, surviving as roughly a thousand animals across seven scattered micro-populations. San Parks entrusted his care to conservationist Brett Barlow at Founders Lodge in the Eastern Cape, on condition the project work toward breeding him.

"We're down to every last individual matters."

Black rhino, white rhino

The name is a historical accident. Dutch settlers described the grazing, square-lipped rhino as the "wyd," or wide-mouth, which English speakers heard as "white"; the browsing species, with its narrower, prehensile lip built for pulling leaves off branches, became "black" by default rather than by any difference in colour. White rhino are the larger of the two, bulls running up to 500 kilograms heavier than a black rhino bull, and far more placid. Black rhino carry a reputation for aggression serious enough that they, not their larger cousins, are the rhino counted among the traditional Big Five, a list originally drawn up around which animals were hardest to hunt safely on foot. Borchett is direct about the risk: he has had a friend killed by a black rhino, and says a bull can close 100 metres faster than Usain Bolt can run it, fast enough to flip a loaded Land Cruiser if it connects.

White rhino nearly disappeared entirely. A century and a half ago Kruger alone held numbers in the hundreds of thousands; by the early twentieth century poaching had reduced the population to perhaps fifty animals nationally. Dr. Ian Player, brother of golfer Gary Player, is credited with saving them, dispersing rhino to zoos and parks around the world to rebuild a breeding base that eventually recovered to roughly 25,000. The recent poaching wave has cut deep into that recovery: Kruger alone has lost around 80 percent of its white rhino, and the national total is now estimated at about 15,000. Some reserves have stopped publicising whether they hold rhino at all, or how many, purely as a security measure. At sanctuaries further along than the Shannon Elizabeth Foundation's own, an orphaned calf that loses its mother to poaching is sometimes given an emotional-support companion to stop it grieving alone: a goat for a black rhino calf, since goats browse the way black rhino do, or a sheep for a white rhino calf, which grazes.

The bravest person he's met

Borchett is candid that a lot of foreign money into African conservation carries what he calls a white-saviour undertone: wealthy outsiders arriving to tell local conservationists what to do. Shannon Elizabeth's approach, in his telling, was the opposite. Before committing to anything, she spent time travelling through Kenya, Malawi, Zimbabwe and South Africa on her own money, sitting with conservationists on the ground and learning what the actual problems were. The decision to move crystallised on a street corner in New York: standing at a crossing on Fifth Avenue, watching the crowd flood past wrapped up in themselves, she decided the life she had didn't have a purpose. She put her belongings in storage, packed her dog, and moved to Africa knowing exactly one person, a contact named Jana Pretorius who met her at the airport.

It wasn't her first act of philanthropy. Around 2001, not long after she'd found fame in Hollywood, Shannon started a dog and cat rescue called Animal Avengers, its logo drawn by a friend who is now Marvel's creative director. She later trademarked the name herself. When she and Borchett got together, they decided to commit fully to conservation, brought his semi-retired father in as chairman, and spent roughly the first four years deliberately learning where the foundation could add the most value rather than picking a cause and forcing it: dehorning programmes, canine anti-poaching units, education and scholarships, and for a while the largest online rhino-information portal in the world, before the work eventually led them to Munu.

The foundation's fundraising has its own momentum now. Each year during the World Series of Poker's roughly ten-week run in Las Vegas, Borchett organises a charity tournament timed to Day 1A of the main event, when the tournament's biggest crowd of players is in the city. They expected around fifty people the first year; 115 showed up, several of them bracelet winners who flew in purely to support Shannon rather than to play the main event itself. A photograph sold on the night of the first tournament, and the event now repeats annually, with Peter half-joking he'll fly out as hand luggage for the next one.

900 hectares of raw thicket

To give Munu a real breeding sanctuary rather than a holding pen, the foundation secured 900 hectares of untouched Albany thicket just west of Addo, prime black rhino country that had never been built on. Borchett describes staking out the ground for Munu's boma with wooden pegs on the first morning after getting the keys, before roads, water, solar power or cabins existed. Peter camped there with him on the third night, before Munu himself had even arrived on the land, out on raw, pathless thicket that took 45 minutes to cross from the front gate by vehicle.
