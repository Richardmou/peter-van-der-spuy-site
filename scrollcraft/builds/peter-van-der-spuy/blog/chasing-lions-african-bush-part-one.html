Chasing lions and adventures in the African bush, part one

Wildlife filmmaker Robert Hofmeyer on the lion pride that plays two rival coalitions against each other, the camera rig built to film them from ground level, and the night one of them ate his camping chair.

Peter van der Spuy Adapted from the podcast episode, with Robert Hofmeyer

"I have been charged by an angry male lion," is how Robert Hofmeyer opens the conversation. It is not the last time in this episode that a lion gets the better of him.

Hofmeyer is a wildlife filmmaker based in Hermanus who works mostly across the border in Botswana. For the past few years his focus has narrowed to almost exclusively lions and leopards, filming alongside fellow videographer Pablo, an American who moved to South Africa for its wildlife and has spent recent years documenting conservation efforts, including shark research, across the continent. Pablo's own work leans more toward the human side of conservation, the people and effort behind saving a species rather than the animal alone, but he says what pulled him toward South Africa specifically, more than the wildlife itself, was the people he met once he got there. He first found Hofmeyer online, admiring the rigged Land Cruiser door he'd engineered for eye-level filming, and reached out purely to say so. Hofmeyer, despite the rig being a personal prototype he'd built for no one but himself, met him in Cape Town on Kloof Street and offered to help him build his own version anyway, no obligation attached. Pablo credits exactly that kind of generosity, more than the animals, with why he stayed.

The pride that plays both sides

Hofmeyer has been tracking one particular pride since 2021, filming them almost full time for about the last two years: a group of lionesses in Chobe National Park along the riverfront, living at the exact point where the territories of two rival male coalitions meet. The pride's solution is tactical. The lionesses mate with males from both coalitions, so each side has reason to believe the cubs might be its own, and neither kills them.

Hofmeyer calls the group the Misfits, and its clearest character is a lioness he first filmed in 2021, Broken Paw, so badly injured in one paw at the time that she could barely put weight on it. Unable to keep pace on hunts, she settled into the role of the pride's babysitter, watching cubs while the others hunted; lions aren't altruistic the way dogs can be, so the pride never brought food back to her, only allowed her to feed at a kill if she managed to catch up, which she sometimes failed to do for a week at a stretch, left to scavenge or steal scraps from leopard kills instead. About two years before this recording, despite the injury, she had cubs of her own.

The Chobe pride has also never had to learn to fear people the way lions elsewhere do. With a permanent river nearby, they have no need to approach campsites for water, and by Hofmeyer's account they are more frightened of humans on foot than curious about vehicles. "If I were to get out of the vehicle, they would run away for sure," he says.

A lens worth more than the car

Filming lions at eye level took a purpose-built rig. Hofmeyer modified a Land Cruiser door himself: widening the window opening, replacing the glass with an aluminum plate, and mounting the camera on a magnetic plate that pushes outward on a parallelogram arm, dropping the lens to about 60 centimeters above the ground. Shooting from below the animals rather than down onto them, he says, is what actually reads as immersive on camera.

The camera itself is a RED V-Raptor, with a Nikon ZR as a low-light backup. The lens is a Canon CN20, a fully manual 50 to 1,000mm zoom with no autofocus and no image stabilization, built specifically for wildlife work. It is the only lens of its kind Canon makes, and there are only five or six units in South Africa, most of them held as rental stock. Hofmeyer bought his outright because months at a time in the field make renting impractical.

"The lens is worth more than my car."

The night a lion ate his camping chair

The best story in the episode happened at a campsite in the Kalahari. A young male lion, habituated by other campers who illegally leave out water bowls to draw lions in for a closer sighting, wandered into Hofmeyer's tent looking for the water it had come to expect. Finding none, it worked through the tent's contents instead: first a pillow, dragged off into the grass and chewed apart, then a full sleeping bag pulled out by the mouth, and finally the camping chair, which it ate outright. Hofmeyer and Pablo, sitting in the vehicle at the time, hooted the horn and revved the engine trying to make it drop the sleeping bag.

Hofmeyer's read on it is straightforward: the lion wasn't hunting, it was checking a campsite that had trained it to expect a reward, and when the reward wasn't there, it took what it could find instead. He filed what he calls "a very interesting insurance claim." Neither of them fed the lion; the damage came purely from an animal returning to a place it had already learned, wrongly, to associate with an easy meal. The lesson he draws from it is blunt.

Don't feed the animals.

The Okavango charge that opens the episode came from a different lesson entirely. Hofmeyer had gotten closer than he should have to a male mating with a female, and after days of guarding her, the male had had enough. "He got angry, and he went at me," Hofmeyer says. "I was very disrespectful of him, and I think that is why. I've been taught a lesson." He was inside the vehicle when it happened, which is, by his account, the only reason lions tend not to see the humans riding around inside one as separate from the vehicle itself, and therefore not as something edible. Step out, and that changes immediately, a distinction Hofmeyer treats as the single most important rule of working this close to an animal that could kill him in seconds if it chose to.
