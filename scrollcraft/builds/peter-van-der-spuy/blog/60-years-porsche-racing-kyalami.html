Motorsport / Circuit History

60 years of Porsche racing at Kyalami

Dennis Klopper, author of the book on Kyalami's history, on the 700-million-rand rebuild that saved the circuit, the auction that nearly lost it, and what it would actually take to bring Formula 1 back.

Peter van der Spuy Adapted from the podcast episode, with Dennis Klopper

Dennis Klopper has been going to Kyalami since he was six years old, watching his first Grand Prix there as a spectator in the late 1960s. He never missed a race after that, and in 2004 became the circuit's general manager, a role he held through its near-collapse and eventual reconstruction. He is the author of a book on 60 years of Porsche racing at the circuit.

The auction that saved it

By the early 2010s, Kyalami had gone at least two years without proper management and had fallen into serious decline, passing through a run of owners that included the Theodosiou family before it was put up for sale. In 2014 it went to auction for a reported 205 million rand, bought by Toby Venter, owner of Porsche South Africa. What followed was not a refresh but a full reconstruction: the entire circuit surface was stripped, with the old tar repurposed to resurface the venue's side roads, roughly 100,000 old tires were pulled out and replaced, and kilometers of fencing and cabling went in new. Klopper estimates the total investment, between circuit and facility, at somewhere between 600 and 700 million rand.

"He's a man of vision, and without his investment, this circuit wouldn't exist today."

A circuit that size does not pay for itself through racing alone, which is why the rebuild included conference and event facilities alongside the track. "You can't just rely on motorsport," Klopper says. "You've got to create a multi-faceted venue." It is, by his account, the only way Kyalami, or any circuit like it, stays solvent. Klopper worked the reconstruction itself alongside Andrew Baldwin, who had helped build out Porsche's dealership network in South Africa for Venter, and with the FIA's Charlie Whiting on bringing the circuit up to international grading.

A book that took almost a lifetime

The book itself grew out of a single line Venter said to Klopper on a visit to the track: everyone has a Kyalami memory, whether they've been there once or a hundred times. Klopper took that as the premise for a book on 60 years of the circuit's history, a project that, in his own telling, has effectively occupied much of his working life. It was meant to launch in 2021, on the actual 60th anniversary of the circuit's 1961 opening, and slipped well past that, partly because Klopper kept wanting to add more to it before letting it go to print. The finished edition ran to 100 signed, numbered copies. Peter's is number eight, the same number carried by his own race car, the one that appears elsewhere on this site, and he calls owning the matching pair, book and car sharing a number by pure coincidence, one of the small details that made the gift land harder than a signed book alone would have, the tribute to the 1974 Carrera RSR Turbo, the factory's first turbocharged racing car, a 2.1-litre, 500-horsepower build weighing around 900 kilograms that took him roughly eight years to complete to original specification. Klopper invited him to bring that car to Kyalami's Festival of Motoring as a guest for a classic parade, in 2017 or 2019 by his recollection, and the signed copy of the book he later gave Peter came paired with a photograph of him in the pits with his crew that day.

The book also carries a signed contribution from Venter himself, tied to the car he raced at Kyalami for years: a 1993 Porsche 911 GT2 R Turbo that, between its dominance in period and the sheer length of its competitive career, holds more outright race victories than any other race car in South African motorsport, not merely class wins. At its peak it was, by Klopper's description, the most photographed Porsche on the circuit.

Sixty years of one marque winning

Porsche's history at Kyalami goes back to the circuit's opening race in 1961, the Kyalami Nine Hour, won outright by a Porsche 550. Porsche returned to win the modern revival of the same race in 2019 with a GT car, a fitting bookend for a book built around the marque's history there. In between sits a run of wins that includes David Piper's victory in a Porsche 917, only the second win anywhere in the world for that chassis. Piper, still visiting South Africa regularly with his wife Liz, wrote one of the book's forewords; Le Mans legend Derek Bell wrote another.

The most personal history in the book, for Peter, belongs to Sarel van der Merwe, the twelve-time South African rally champion who also raced at Le Mans for factory teams eight or nine times and won the Daytona 24 Hours outright alongside Tony Martin and Graham Duxbury in a Porsche-powered March. Peter grew up idolising him before ever racing against him, and did eventually get the chance, in an endurance race at Phakisa: Peter in a quicker 997 Cup car, van der Merwe in a Ginetta, out-driving the pace disadvantage lap after lap in a battle Peter still calls a fair, hard-fought match. Peter's own car failed partway through, and he stayed to watch van der Merwe finish, greeting him at the car exhausted and soaked in sweat with a hug he admits brought him to tears, the two of them having sung the national anthem together before the race began.

What it would take to bring Formula 1 back

Kyalami currently holds FIA Grade 2 certification, one level below the Grade 1 standard Formula 1 requires, but Klopper is clear that grading the circuit further would be pointless without a confirmed race deal first. The circuit surface itself is close to F1-ready; what it lacks sits mostly around the edges, technical pit infrastructure and the grandstands and spectator facilities the sport now expects, none of which Kyalami's post-reconstruction design prioritised. Hosting a race also depends on three separate parties each needing to be paid: Formula 1 wants a rights fee for bringing the event, the venue wants a fee for hosting it, and a promoter sits in between trying to generate enough revenue to cover both, in a sport where F1 itself retains most of the income. Crowd numbers make or break that model. Recent Formula 1 weekends draw 300,000 to 400,000 spectators, with Silverstone approaching half a million, and Kyalami, designed around grass embankments rather than permanent grandstands, would need substantial temporary seating brought in just to compete on capacity.
