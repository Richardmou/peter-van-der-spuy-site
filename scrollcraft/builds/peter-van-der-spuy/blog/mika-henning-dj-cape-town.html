Music / DJ Culture

Inside the mind of a top female DJ

Hamburg DJ Mika Henning on a failed first Cape Town set, the residency at Noname Bar that changed everything, and the New Year's house party the police shut down.

Peter van der Spuy Adapted from the podcast episode, with Mika Henning

Mika Henning, from Hamburg, came to DJing sideways. She spent years as a producer and casting director, including seven years living in Miami, before deciding, once that career had run its course, to finally chase the thing she'd wanted to do since she could think. She started in 2015, by her own description completely naive about the business side of it, playing for the love of the music with no real plan. This episode itself began as a deliberate idea from co-host Mikey Kay, pitched to Peter over beers as a female-DJs strand for the show, with Henning suggested as its first guest, a choice Peter needed no convincing on given how long he'd already been hearing about her from mutual friends before ever seeing her play.

A locked-out laptop and a friend's empty bar

Her first real gig came from persistence rather than a booking. She asked a friend with a restaurant in Hamburg if she could set up her laptop in his unused downstairs bar just to hear her mixes loud, in a real room instead of through headphones. She played for hours, alone, and he listened from upstairs. Two weeks later he asked her to headline an after-work club session, technical skills or not. She said yes and figured out the equipment as she went, on a small consumer DJ controller she later realised should never have been let anywhere near the room's real sound system. An established local DJ booked to support that night dismissed her before he'd even heard her play; partway through the set he changed his mind enough to ask about booking her again. Her third gig ever was at one of Hamburg's best-known clubs. She credits not following or comparing herself to other DJs at the time, a naivety she now calls the actual secret to sounding like herself rather than an imitation of someone else.

The Cape Town set that didn't happen, and the one that did

Henning's international career took off later, in Cape Town, though not on her first attempt. In 2016, out with friends at a small new venue called Noname Bar, she told the owner, Justin, that she was a DJ and got offered a chance to play; she froze, hit the wrong button, killed her own sound, and admitted she wasn't ready. She came back the following year having actually prepared, ran into Justin again at a pool in Camps Bay, and told him she was ready this time. He gave her a quiet Tuesday slot. It didn't stay quiet. Within a run of Tuesdays she'd become the venue's main Thursday-night DJ, the night she also met her partner Matteo, an Italian from Venice who came to support a friend and stayed.

"If you get a nice feedback from your crowd, you can play forever."

Word of mouth from those nights led to New Year's Eve bookings, playing the melodic house and techno she loves most, then a New Year's Day house party where a group visiting from London paid her whatever she asked to play again the next morning. She played loud, as she's known for. The police shut the party down, and the hosts, renting the house, were evicted the following day over neighbour complaints. Henning had already taken her fee and left an hour and a half earlier. She still returns to Cape Town for a few months most years, and Peter, who had turned down Matteo's invitations to come hear her play more than once before finally going, has since watched her DJ private parties in the wealthy Clifton and Fresnaye areas of the city, calling her stage presence unusually understated for the scene: someone playing music in her own vein rather than performing a persona.

The work nobody sees on the night

Henning is direct that the actual work of DJing is almost entirely invisible to an audience: building new sounds and assembling a set is where most of the hours go, not the performance itself. Her own routine runs on a fixed rhythm, prepared well ahead of a gig and largely finished two days out, followed by a day of listening to nothing at all before she plays. She is equally direct about a male-dominated industry that still, in her view, judges women DJs on appearance in a way it doesn't judge men, who can perform in a plain t-shirt and be assessed purely on the music. Getting ready before a set, she says, carries an extra layer most male DJs never have to think about at all: what to wear, how to look, on top of everything else the job already demands, an imbalance she doesn't expect to see corrected any time soon, but names plainly rather than pretending it isn't there.

Henning is also deliberate about what she'll say no to. She has turned down well-paid private bookings outright rather than play generic wedding staples outside her own melodic house and techno identity, on the reasoning that compromising the set actually damages her as a performer rather than just costing her a fee. Being an all-rounder who plays whatever a client wants was never the goal; being known for a specific sound, even if it cost her some bookings early on, was. She defines "melodic" simply: always a melody running through the track rather than a flat, repetitive beat, often built around a vocal, and she suspects that instinct for melody over monotony is part of why she rates so many of the other women DJing alongside her right now.

Asked what actually separates a good DJ from a great one, her answer is immediate: a good DJ plays music people want to hear; a great one plays music people didn't know they wanted to hear. She's equally clear-eyed about the economics behind that standard. DJing is a low-paying trade for almost everyone doing it, a small handful of globally recognised names, Black Coffee among them, who the hosts note recently bought one of the most expensive houses in Cape Town, aside. With no agency working on her behalf, she runs her own Instagram, Facebook, TikTok and SoundCloud presence herself on top of finding and preparing music, which is why she describes a second job on the side, something most working DJs need, as something that would cost her the time she can't actually spare.
