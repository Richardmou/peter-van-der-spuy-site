Motorsport / Rivalry

F1, rally and rivalries: untold stories, part one

Sarel van der Merwe and Ian Scheckter, two South African racing legends and one-time rivals, on rallying an Audi in the Group B era, an F1 practice session stopped by a fatal crash, and how a Daytona campaign turned enemies into teammates.

Peter van der Spuy Adapted from the podcast episode, with Sarel van der Merwe and Ian Scheckter

Sarel van der Merwe and Ian Scheckter do not need an introduction, and Peter, with co-host Leon Bosman, does not try to give them one. "If we had to roll out all the accolades and achievements, we're going to be at least an hour," Peter says. Instead the conversation goes looking for the stories that do not make the record books: the ones neither driver, both of whom are also pilots, has told on a podcast before. Peter draws a direct line between the two disciplines from his own experience: when he first got into racing around thirteen or fourteen years before this recording, competitors kept assuming he'd raced before until he told them he flew for a living, at which point the reaction was always the same, that it made sense, given how used a pilot already is to judging speed and depth at 250 km/h on every landing.

A coffin on wheels

Ian Scheckter, younger brother of 1979 Formula 1 world champion Jody Scheckter, raced South African-spec Formula 1 through the 1970s, a category built around single-seaters rather than the international championship. His first F1 practice session, delayed by the 1973 global fuel crisis before it could even happen, ended before he left the pits: a fellow driver was killed on track. "I was sitting in the car thinking, this is a coffin on wheels. I could be dead in ten minutes," he says. He draws a direct line to today's cars, built around a survival cell that lets the chassis break apart while the driver walks away. In his era, the cars were aluminum. "They just crumble," he says, "like an aeroplane when it crashes."

A Lotus bought straight from the factory

Scheckter's first Formula 1 car was a genuine Lotus 72, the same chassis Emerson Fittipaldi won a world championship in, bought directly from the factory at the end of a season, a practice that existed then and simply doesn't now. He's blunt about what separates his era's fitness demands from today's: modern drivers train like professional athletes because the downforce and G-forces genuinely require it, on contracts worth tens of millions a year. In his day, preparation was closer to incidental. He trained through surf lifesaving club conditioning and road running with a rugby club, and remembers Ronnie Peterson describing his own training regimen as pulling a breeze block from under his bed, running around it, and getting back in. Still, Scheckter found the open cockpits and shorter, harder-charging races of the period physically brutal in their own right, just differently from how modern F1 is, the airflow alone through an open cockpit doing to the body what modern G-forces do through a closed one, just through a different kind of punishment entirely.

Racing internationally during that era carried a political cost South African drivers had to navigate directly. With the country under sporting boycotts through apartheid, entering some countries to compete meant concealing the actual purpose of the trip, travelling in on something closer to a tourist pretext and leaving again immediately after racing. Japan was particularly strict about it. Scheckter got in, raced, and was told afterward he would never be allowed back into the country, a real professional cost for an era of drivers who are otherwise remembered mostly for the racing itself.

The Audi that felt like nothing else

Van der Merwe's career ran through rally before it ran through circuit racing, and he is unambiguous about which he preferred. "It was more fun than the racing cars," he says of rallying, "because you were always on a road you'd never been on before." Of every car he drove, he names the Group B-era Audi, driven on turbocharged all-wheel-drive rally stages, as the most satisfying: solid, quick, and exactly as capable as he expected it to be every time he got in it. He also rallied in the United States, not only on South African stages. Racing a road that's always new, often at night, taught him to solve problems in real time in a way circuit racing on a fixed, memorised track never demanded, a skill he says carried directly into how quickly he could learn an unfamiliar circuit once he moved into tarmac racing.

"That was probably the nicest one, and the easiest one."

Enemies, then teammates, at Daytona

Both drivers raced Daytona together, but not always on the same side. From 1980 to 1984 they were rivals in South African silhouette racing, a rivalry Peter and Leon trace back to a well-known flashpoint remembered simply as "barbecue bend." It took a team meeting, engineered to see whether the two could actually work together, to turn them into teammates. Scheckter is blunt about the early tension: "He tried to assassinate me," he says of van der Merwe, laughing, before describing one of their first shared races at Road America, one of his favourite circuits, where van der Merwe ran the opening stint after a safety-car restart determined to break away from Derek Bell's Porsche 956. He did, building a 15-second lead within 15 minutes, before endurance racing caught up with him. "After fifteen, twenty minutes I was knackered," he says, "and I spun and didn't know why." He spun a second time a lap later, still without knowing the cause, still holding the lead regardless; the third spin had a clear explanation, another car making contact with his, which finally forced him into the pits.

Both are in their late seventies now (van der Merwe turns 80 on the 5th of December, Scheckter is a year behind him) and both say outright that a racing driver never really retires, only slows down. Van der Merwe still gets invited to fix and test other people's cars, and admits he recently ran a few laps at Kyalami purely for himself. "I thought, I must come back and race again," he says. "My wife nearly had a heart attack."

The episode ends with both men driving a hot lap in a BMW M4 race car around a simulated Monza, neither having raced there before, against a standing, unbeaten reference time set anonymously by a driver the show calls The Twig, on identical car settings and track conditions with no one told in advance which combination they'd get.
