Alpine Cup driver and race coach reveal endurance secrets

22-year-old Alpine Cup driver Louis Maurice, raised trackside by a father with three decades of racing, and coach Claudio Piazza Musso on leading Killarney's six-hour with three laps to go.

Peter van der Spuy Adapted from the podcast episode, with Louis Maurice and Claudio Piazza Musso

Louis Maurice is 22, French, from near Lille in the north of the country, and grew up literally around race paddocks. His father has raced for three decades, across rallying and endurance events including several years contesting the VLN championship at the Nürburgring when Louis was around ten. Before Louis ever sat in a kart, his father let him lap the family's own 997 GT3 Porsche on the sim rig at home, so by the time an actual kart test came around he already had a rough feel for a car at speed. Louis started karting relatively late by European standards, in 2017 at 15, not because his family lacked racing pedigree but because his father, deep in rally and circuit racing himself, had no particular background in karting to pass on. His first session, on a small track near home, ran in 8-degree rain. He finished so far off the pace that his father wondered if they'd simply picked a bad day. Louis kept at it through the winter, training with a regional team through March and April, and closed his first competitive season having started dead last and finished inside the top five of twenty drivers.

One car, one setup, all driver

Maurice currently races in the Alpine Cup, a European one-make series run in the Alpine A110, organised by the manufacturer itself. In a one-make series every car on the grid is mechanically identical, with only minor, tightly limited suspension and setup adjustments allowed, which strips the result down to almost pure driver skill rather than equipment. He calls himself a genuine fan of the format for exactly that reason.

His coach, Claudio Piazza Musso, describes karting itself as the foundation everything else is built on, a discipline with no suspension at all, so the driver feels the tyre directly rather than through anti-roll bars and dampers. Learning to keep a tyre in its narrow working range there, he says, is what later lets a driver give an engineer useful feedback on a full-sized race car's setup. Karting also runs on strict minimum-weight rules, checked on the scales immediately after every race, which makes every gram a live tactical question: a lightweight junior driver at Piazza Musso's own team once came in three kilograms over his class's minimum with the kart itself, and the team shaved the difference by stripping the sticker kit for a kilogram and removing the footrest hardware for another half, working down to the exact limit before the next race. It is the same instinct, on a smaller scale, that stripped the paint off 1930s Mercedes and Auto Union grand prix cars to make national weight limits, leaving bare aluminium bodywork that gave the cars their name: the Silver Arrows.

Three laps from a win

Out in South Africa for a few months, Maurice got the chance to race the six-hour endurance round at Killarney in Cape Town, sharing a car with coach and team manager Claudio Piazza Musso, who runs one of the country's leading racing teams. The pair were leading with three laps remaining before faster GT3 machinery caught and passed them, dropping the result to fourth, decided by a matter of seconds rather than a clear gap.

"We're leading with three laps to go, and then the GT3 cars were just too fast, and we couldn't keep them behind."

Piazza Musso's role on the weekend covered the side of endurance racing that never makes the results sheet: pit strategy, driver debriefs, and the preparation that has to be finished before the race starts, since in a six-hour format there is no real time to fix a mistake once the lights go out. He is candid about his own start in the sport: coming into circuit racing late himself, through a Porsche Cup car, and being unable to understand how lighter drivers in slower-looking machinery kept passing him on pace alone. Maurice's counterpoint is that carrying extra weight can force a driver to protect momentum more carefully through a corner, discipline a lighter, more forgiving car lets you get away without.

Piazza Musso sees the same raw material across every driver who has come through the podcast's own hot-lap segment, whether racing drivers, helicopter pilots or safari guides: not raw horsepower under them, but hand-eye coordination built somewhere else first, in a simulator, a cockpit, or years of picking a line through rocks in a 4x4. Most newcomers, he says, mistake a fast car for fast driving. What actually separates a quick lap from a slow one is learning to turn properly in the first place, the same lesson karting teaches at eight or ten years old in Europe, and the same lesson Maurice worked through in the cold and the rain outside Lille at fifteen.

Braking a full 100 metres earlier

Piazza Musso's coaching method with a first-time track driver is almost always the same. He lets them run two or three laps to settle in, watching from the passenger seat until they reach the point where pushing any harder would put the car off the track. Then he takes over. Driving the same slow laps, he starts braking a full 100 metres earlier than the student had been, at a reference point well before Kyalami's exit from pit lane. It looks over-cautious at low speed, but as he builds the pace back up lap by lap, the same early braking point turns out to be exactly where a car actually needs to start slowing to make the corner without drama. Handed the car back, most students brake noticeably later than they had before, chasing the feel they'd just seen.

The physics lesson usually follows within a lap or two, once a student starts braking hard and late in something like a 1.9-ton V8 BMW M3: the pedal begins to shudder as the fluid nears boiling point, an early warning of brake fade that most road drivers have never felt on purpose. Piazza Musso is blunt about who this applies to. It isn't only inexperienced young drivers in quick first cars; it's just as often someone who spent years in an ordinary hatchback, came into money later in life, and walked straight into a dealership for a 450-horsepower Porsche Carrera without ever learning what a car with that much performance actually demands of a driver. He half-jokes that this kind of training should be compulsory for anyone buying their first genuinely fast car, not an optional extra.
