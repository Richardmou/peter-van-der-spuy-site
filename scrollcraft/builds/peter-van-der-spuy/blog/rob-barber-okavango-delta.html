The wildest luxury safari in Africa?

Safari guide Rob Barber on the Okavango Delta's rare inland geography, 21 years running mobile camps there, and the closest of close calls with a charging hippo.

Peter van der Spuy Adapted from the podcast episode, with Rob Barber

Rob Barber was born in Tanzania and educated in Britain, and by his own account could not leave East Africa quickly enough once he was old enough to choose. What he found instead, 21 years ago, was the Okavango Delta in northern Botswana, and he never left. He started as a driver and guide for someone else's operation before building his own, Golden Africa Safaris, a mobile safari operation renamed from its original Golden Okavango in 2012. The business has since taken clients on ad hoc trips as far afield as Madagascar, Ethiopia, Gabon, Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Rwanda, Zimbabwe, Zambia and Namibia, though Barber himself still spends roughly three-quarters of his working time in the Okavango, the corner of the continent he never actually meant to stay in.

A river that goes nowhere

Botswana is roughly the size of France or Texas, around 600,000 square kilometres, and the Okavango Delta covers about 25,000 square kilometres of it in the country's northwest. What makes it geographically unusual is that it is endorheic: unlike exorheic rivers such as the Limpopo, the Zambezi, the Thames or the Hudson, all of which eventually reach an ocean, the Okavango River flows inland and simply spreads out and stops, never reaching the sea. That single fact of geography is what created one of the largest inland wetland systems on the continent, with at least eight or nine distinct habitat types inside it.

The river itself starts in Angola and crosses into Botswana through Namibia's Caprivi Strip still contained in a single channel with proper banks, a stretch known as the panhandle for its shape on a map, like the handle of a frying pan attached to the delta's fan. A couple of hundred kilometres in, it crosses the Gomare fault line, where the land on either side sits higher than the land the water occupies, and that is where the river stops behaving like a river and starts spreading into the floodplains and papyrus reed beds that define the delta proper. The whole system sits ringed by the dry Kalahari sand basin, which is precisely what has kept it so undisturbed: Barber describes riding into it and seeing terrain that reads exactly as it must have for millions of years, an island of permanent water and enormous trees held inside a desert.

Tens of thousands of zebra in a day

The Okavango region hosts the second-largest large-mammal migration in Africa, a plain-zebra migration involving two distinct subspecies that start from different points and meet in the middle. One population begins around the Okavango Delta, the Linyanti and Chobe systems; the other starts further south along the Boteti River. When the rains arrive, both head toward the northern Makgadikgadi Pans for the fresh grass, converging on the same grasslands from opposite directions at once. On the right day, Barber says, you can watch tens of thousands of zebra in the same field of view.

A tent, not a lodge

Barber's operating philosophy runs against the instinct most camps have to keep adding more: more furnishings, more fixtures, more separation from the actual bush outside. His approach is closer to just enough. Guests get a proper double bed, a bronze wash basin with hot water heated over an outside fire, solar-charged lighting strung around the tent, cold drinks and food good enough that visiting guests have asked how it's possible to cook it that well in the middle of nowhere, and nothing beyond that. The point of the comfort, in his telling, is to remove any reason to think about comfort at all, so the only thing left to focus on is the Delta itself.

What makes a mobile camp different from a fixed lodge, he argues, is that a canvas wall is not a real barrier the way a lodge's brick and glass are. You're inside the bush rather than observing it from a controlled distance. Simon Borchett found this out directly on a stay at one of Barber's camps: waking early, he looked out through the tent's mesh window to find a hyena sitting a few feet away, mid-toilet, who glanced up at him, finished, and wandered off. Peter has stayed at some of the continent's best-known reserves, Serengeti, Gorongosa, Kruger, Phinda and Etosha among them, and says that asked which one he would choose for a last safari, without hesitation, it would be Barber's camp in the Okavango.

Too stupid to stop

Barber's closest call in the bush did not come from lion or elephant. On an island in the Zambezi, dense enough with vegetation to have only a single path through it, doubling as what he calls a hippo highway, he glanced back to find a hippo roughly 10 metres behind a colleague, Sean, both of them running. His read on the danger was immediate and specific.

"You can reason with a lion. You can reason with an elephant. Hippo and buffalo, you just run. They're too stupid to stop."

Picturing the path as a clockface, with the group running toward 12 and the hippo closing from 6, Barber cut back past it toward 5 o'clock and a gap in the undergrowth, calling for Sean to turn with him. Sean turned too, but the hippo carried on straight down the original path rather than following either of them. It is, Barber says, the closest he has come in 21 years.

Barber's read on animal behaviour comes from two decades of watching the same stretch of land, not textbook knowledge. Wildlife photographer Simon Borchett, who has tracked leopard alongside him, describes Barber's ability with something close to awe: he can look at an individual animal in the bush and identify which family line it belongs to, effectively knowing its grandmother by sight. That kind of pattern recognition, built purely from repetition and 21 years in the same delta, is what let him read the hippo's charge correctly in the seconds it mattered, rather than guessing.

At the time of the conversation, Golden Africa Safaris was shortlisted for Best Mobile Safari in Africa at the 2025 Safari Awards in Durban, the same year Barber marked two decades running the business under one name or another. He still describes the Delta, after 21 years living inside it, the way a first-time visitor might: as somewhere he genuinely never wanted to leave.
